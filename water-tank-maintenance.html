How to Clean a Plastic Water Tank

Plan safe draining, debris removal, cleaning, rinsing and contamination prevention according to the water use and current public-health guidance.

Inspect before deciding to clean

Look for sediment, unusual odour, visible contamination, damaged covers, insect entry points or dirty inlet screens. Cleaning frequency depends on water source and system condition.

Isolate and drain safely

Shut off inflow and pumps where necessary. Plan where the drained water will go so it does not damage foundations, slopes or neighbouring property.

Remove sediment and clean surfaces

Use cleaning methods and products compatible with the tank material and intended water use. Avoid tools that scratch or damage the inside surface.

Rinse and restore the system

Remove cleaning residue and check that covers, screens, overflow and inlet components return to secure positions before normal use.

Prevent contamination between cleanings

Keep lids closed, maintain gutter screens for rainwater systems, prevent animals and insects from accessing openings and repair damaged plumbing.

When to seek professional help

Large tanks, confined access, suspected contamination or systems supplying sensitive users need a safer professional approach. Do not enter a tank without appropriate confined-space safety controls.

Cleaning frequency depends on the system

There is no single cleaning interval that fits every tank. A sealed tank receiving treated municipal water may remain cleaner than a rainwater tank receiving roof runoff. Inspection is therefore more useful than blindly following a fixed calendar. Check sediment, screens, lids, inlets and water condition, then schedule cleaning based on the actual system.

Rainwater systems need upstream maintenance

Cleaning the tank while ignoring dirty gutters and failed screens solves only part of the problem. Keep roof catchments, gutters, first-flush devices and inlet screens maintained. After a long dry period, accumulated roof debris can contaminate the first rainfall if the system is not prepared.

Confined-space safety

Large tanks are not ordinary rooms. Entry into enclosed tanks creates serious confined-space hazards, including poor air quality and difficult rescue. Avoid entering a tank unless trained professionals use the correct procedures and safety equipment. Many cleaning tasks should be performed without entry.
